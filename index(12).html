Historical Context

Piper TTS represents an important milestone in open-source neural text-to-speech technology. Developed as a fast, local, and privacy-focused alternative to cloud-based TTS services, Piper brings high-quality neural voice synthesis to edge devices and offline applications.

Each Piper voice model is trained on carefully curated datasets, supporting over 40 languages with varying quality levels optimized for different use cases — from resource-constrained IoT devices to high-fidelity audiobook narration. The project emphasizes accessibility, enabling developers to integrate natural-sounding speech synthesis without relying on proprietary cloud services.

Preserving these voice models ensures continued access to open-source TTS technology and documents the evolution of community-driven neural voice synthesis systems.

Submit a Voice

The Virtual Voice Archive depends on contributions from the community to grow and stay complete. If you have a virtual voice that you'd like to see preserved here, we want to hear from you.

How to Submit

Send an email to the archive with any voices you'd like to contribute. Please include as much detail as you have — the voice name, the system it belongs to, the language, the original release date, and any sample audio files or download links you're able to share.

All submissions are reviewed before being added to the archive. There are no strict formatting requirements — whatever you can provide is helpful. Even a name and a brief description goes a long way toward preservation.

Send to Garduuk@gmail.com

About

Our mission

About the Virtual Voice Archive

The Virtual Voice Archive is a community-maintained digital library dedicated to documenting and preserving non-human synthetic voices — the TTS engines, speech synthesizers, and virtual assistants that shaped how computers learned to speak.

What We Do

We collect, catalogue, and preserve synthetic voices before they are updated, deprecated, or lost to time. Every entry in the archive includes a technical record of the voice's origin, platform, and characteristics, alongside a simulated audio sample to document its sound.

The archive spans more than four decades of speech synthesis history — from 1982 DOS-era formant engines and classic hardware synthesizers to modern open-source neural voice models supporting over one hundred languages.

Why It Matters

Synthetic voices are cultural artefacts. The flat cadence of Microsoft Sam, the resonant bass of DECtalk's Huge Harry, and the whispered breath of Wendy are as much a part of computing history as the hardware they ran on — yet they are routinely lost when software is discontinued or servers shut down.

Preserving these voices keeps the record of human–computer interaction intact. They document how speech synthesis technology evolved, who built it, why it sounded the way it did, and how it shaped the ways people have related to machines over the past half-century.

Scope & Principles

  • The archive covers non-human synthetic voices only — no recordings of human speakers.
  • All entries are documented for educational, historical, and preservation purposes.
  • The archive is static, non-commercial, and carries no advertising.
  • Contributions are welcome from anyone with knowledge of a voice system not yet catalogued.
